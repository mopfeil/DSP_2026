\section*{Background: the 60-2 trigger wheel}

The crank wheel of the virtual engine has 60 tooth positions ($6^\circ$ each),
two of which are missing. The Hall sensor delivers a binary signal; the
rising edge of tooth $k=0,\dots,57$ occurs at $\theta=6k^\circ$ (modulo
$360^\circ$), the reference mark is the first rising edge after the gap.

The tooth period $T_k$ is measured from rising edge to rising edge. Because one
tooth corresponds to $6^\circ = 1/60$ revolution, the engine speed in rpm is
\begin{equation}
  n = \frac{60\,\text{s/min}}{60\,T_k} = \frac{1}{T_k/\text{s}}\ \text{rpm},
  \qquad
  n = \frac{3}{T_{\text{gap}}/\text{s}}\ \text{rpm (gap)},
  \qquad
  n = \frac{60}{T_{\text{rev}}/\text{s}}\ \text{rpm (revolution)}.
  \label{eq:rpm}
\end{equation}


\section*{Background: crankshaft, camshaft and the $720^\circ$ cycle}

A four-stroke engine needs \emph{two} crankshaft revolutions for one working
cycle (intake, compression, power, exhaust), but every valve opens only
once per cycle. The camshaft is therefore driven by a timing belt (or chain)
with a ratio of $2:1$: its sprocket has twice as many teeth as the crankshaft
sprocket, so it turns once while the crankshaft turns twice. The crank angle
$\theta$ is counted over the full cycle, $0^\circ\le\theta<720^\circ$, which
is exactly one camshaft revolution ($360^\circ$ cam angle). The 60-2 wheel on
the crankshaft repeats after $360^\circ$ crank angle, the single segment of
the cam phase wheel only after $720^\circ$.

\begin{solution}
\textbf{a) Classification.} Go through the four criteria one at a time and
ask each time: \emph{what does the sensor deliver}, and \emph{what does the
ECU store}?
\begin{enumerate}[label=Step \arabic*:,leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \emph{Time.} Every sensor delivers a voltage at every instant, so all
    five physical signals are continuous-time (CT). Inside the ECU they become
    discrete-time (DT) signals, in two different ways: analog signals are
    \emph{sampled} at fixed times $t=nT_s$ by the ADC; binary signals are
    \emph{time-stamped} at their edges by a capture timer, so the index is
    the tooth number $k$, not a fixed sampling time.
  \item \emph{Amplitude.} A Hall sensor switches between two levels. This
    makes crank and cam binary. Knock, ion current and lambda are analog
    voltages, and the ADC quantises them (e.g.\ 10 or 12 bits). The lambda
    sensor is analog, but its characteristic is so steep that in practice it
    shows only two values (\SI{0.1}{V} lean, \SI{0.9}{V} rich).
  \item \emph{Predictability.} If the engine speed is known, the tooth wheel
    and the cam pattern follow exactly from the geometry, so they are
    deterministic. Knock and noise have random onset, amplitude and phase, so
    they are stochastic. Combustion varies from cycle to cycle, so the ion
    current is only partly deterministic.
  \item \emph{Periodicity.} Ask after which shift the signal repeats, in time
    $t$ or in crank angle $\theta$: crank $360^\circ$, cam $720^\circ$,
    combustion-related signals $720^\circ/4=180^\circ$ (one event per TDC).
\end{enumerate}
This gives the table (physical signal $\to$ inside the ECU):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.6cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.3cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.3cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
Signal & time & amplitude & predictability & periodicity \\
\midrule
crank & CT $\to$ event times $t_k$ & binary (Hall) & deterministic up to small
  speed fluctuations & periodic in $\theta$ ($360^\circ$); in $t$ only at constant speed \\
cam & CT $\to$ level sampled at tooth events & binary & deterministic &
  periodic in $\theta$ ($720^\circ$) \\
knock & CT $\to$ DT after ADC & analog $\to$ quantised & stochastic (noise,
  random knock onset, amplitude, phase) & quasi-periodic: deterministic parts
  repeat every $180^\circ$, knock is random \\
ion current & CT $\to$ DT & analog & partly deterministic (shape of the
  peaks), cycle-to-cycle variation & aperiodic per event; event pattern
  repeats per $180^\circ$ \\
lambda & CT $\to$ DT (slow, 10--100\,Hz) & analog, but practically
  two-valued (0.1/0.9\,V) & slowly varying, disturbed & aperiodic; in the
  closed loop a limit cycle (quasi-periodic, $\approx$\SI{1}{Hz}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Crank and cam are physically continuous-time voltages with two levels. The
ECU, however, does not sample them. A capture timer time-stamps their edges,
so the information is in the \emph{sequence of edge times} $t_k$, which is a
discrete-time signal indexed by the tooth number. All analog signals become
discrete-time and quantised (= digital) after the ADC.

\textbf{b) Borderline cases.}
(i) A signal is periodic in $t$ if $x(t)=x(t+T)$ for all $t$ and one fixed
$T>0$. During an acceleration $\omega(t)$ increases, so the tooth period
$T_k=6^\circ/\omega$ gets shorter from tooth to tooth and no single $T$
works: the crank signal is \emph{not} periodic in time. Write it as a
function of the crank angle instead, $x(t)=x_c(\theta(t))$ with
$\theta(t)=\int_0^t\omega(t')\dd t'$. The wheel geometry fixes $x_c$, so
$x_c(\theta)=x_c(\theta+360^\circ)$ holds \emph{exactly}, whatever the speed.
This is why many engine algorithms work in the angle domain (Sheet~4).

(ii) Look at a single burst: it is a damped oscillation
$A\,\e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau}\sin(2\pi f_1(t-t_0)+\varphi)$ with random $t_0$, $A$ and
$\varphi$, and in many cycles there is no burst at all. That is a transient,
not a periodic signal. Now look at \emph{averages over many cycles} at a fixed
crank angle: the knock \emph{probability}, the valve impacts, the combustion
vibration and the noise level are the same after every TDC. So the mean
$\mu(\theta)=\mathrm{E}\{x(\theta)\}$ and the autocorrelation
$R(\theta,\theta+\Delta)=\mathrm{E}\{x(\theta)\,x(\theta+\Delta)\}$ satisfy
\[ \mu(\theta+180^\circ)=\mu(\theta),\qquad
   R(\theta+180^\circ,\theta+180^\circ+\Delta)=R(\theta,\theta+\Delta). \]
A signal whose statistics are periodic like this is called
\emph{cyclostationary}. This is exactly what ``quasi-periodic'' means for the
knock signal.

\textbf{c) Time scales at \SI{3000}{rpm}.} Everything follows from two
numbers:
\[ T_{\text{rev}}=\frac{60}{n}=\frac{60}{3000}\,\text{s}=\SI{20}{ms},\qquad
   \omega=\frac{360^\circ}{T_{\text{rev}}}=\SI{18000}{\degree/s}. \]
Each other entry is then an angle divided by $\omega$:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item tooth: $T_z=6^\circ/\omega=\SI{20}{ms}/60=\SI{333.3}{\micro s}$, i.e.\ $f_z=\SI{3}{kHz}$;
  \item gap: the rising edge of tooth 57 is at $342^\circ$, that of tooth 0 at
    $360^\circ$, so $18^\circ/\omega=3\,T_z=\SI{1}{ms}$;
  \item cam period = engine cycle: $720^\circ/\omega=2\,T_{\text{rev}}=\SI{40}{ms}$;
    cam HIGH for $180^\circ$: \SI{10}{ms};
  \item 4 combustions per $720^\circ$: one every $180^\circ$, i.e.\ \SI{10}{ms},
    firing frequency $\SI{100}{Hz}=2\times$ the rotation frequency (2nd order);
  \item $1^\circ$: $1/\omega=\SI{55.6}{\micro s}$, so the knock window
    $5^\circ\dots25^\circ$ ATDC is $\SI{0.278}{ms}\dots\SI{1.389}{ms}$ after TDC;
  \item oscillations within $\tau$: $f_1\tau=\SI{6500}{Hz}\cdot\SI{0.8}{ms}=5.2$.
\end{itemize}

teeth (\SI{300}{\micro s}), combustions (\SI{10}{ms}), lambda (\SI{1}{s}).

\textbf{d) Crank and cam.}
The crank wheel looks identical in both revolutions of a cycle: at
$\theta=120^\circ$ (compression TDC of cylinder~1, ignition) and at
$\theta=480^\circ$ (gas-exchange TDC of cylinder~1, valve overlap) the crank
signal is the same, and so is the piston position. Only the camshaft, which
turns once per cycle, tells the two revolutions apart.

At \SI{200}{rpm}: $T_{\text{rev}}=60/200\,\text{s}=\SI{300}{ms}$, so
$1^\circ\,\widehat{=}\,\SI{0.833}{ms}$.

(i) The ratio test at the reference edge ($360^\circ$) needs \emph{three}
consecutive rising edges: tooth 56 ($336^\circ$) and tooth 57 ($342^\circ$)
give $T_{\text{prev}}$, and tooth 57 and the reference edge give
$T_{\text{gap}}$. The worst case is to switch on just \emph{after} the edge at
$336^\circ$. The current gap then cannot be tested (no $T_{\text{prev}}$), and
the ECU has to wait for the next reference edge at $720^\circ$:
\[ t_{360} = (720^\circ-336^\circ)\cdot\SI{0.833}{ms/\degree}
   = 384^\circ \;\widehat{=}\; \SI{320}{ms}. \]
(Best case: switch on just before $336^\circ$, sync after $24^\circ=\SI{20}{ms}$.)

(ii) After the reference edge the ECU still has to reach the cam evaluation at
$60^\circ$:
\[ t_{720} = 384^\circ+60^\circ=444^\circ \;\widehat{=}\; \SI{370}{ms}. \]
Until then the ECU can use only ``wasted spark'' (fire cylinders 1 and 4
together at every crank TDC), and it must not inject sequentially.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{a) Speed formulas.} Step 1: one tooth is $6^\circ=\frac{1}{60}$\,rev.
If it takes $T_k$ seconds, the speed is
\[ n=\frac{1/60\ \text{rev}}{T_k}=\frac{1}{60\,T_k}\,\frac{\text{rev}}{\text{s}}
    =\frac{60}{60\,T_k}\,\frac{\text{rev}}{\text{min}}=\frac{1}{T_k/\text{s}}\ \text{rpm}. \]
The gap covers $18^\circ=\frac{3}{60}$\,rev and a revolution covers 1\,rev,
so the same steps give $3/T_{\text{gap}}$ and $60/T_{\text{rev}}$.

Step 2, quantisation: the timer can only measure $T_k$ as an integer
multiple of the tick $\Delta$. A period error of one tick causes a speed error
\[ \Delta n\approx\left|\frac{\dd n}{\dd T}\right|\Delta
   =\frac{1}{T^2}\,\Delta = n^2\Delta
   \qquad(n=1/T\text{ in rpm, }T,\Delta\text{ in s}). \]
Example: at \SI{3000}{rpm} and $\Delta=\SI{4}{\micro s}$,
$\Delta n=3000^2\cdot4\cdot10^{-6}=\SI{36}{rpm}$. Equivalently, the relative
error is one tick out of $T_k/\Delta=333/4\approx83$ ticks, i.e.\ 1.2\,\%.

Step 3, compare with the ripple: the model speed is
$\omega=\omega_0(1+\varepsilon\sin2\theta)$ with $\varepsilon=0.01$, i.e.\
amplitude $\pm0.01\,n$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$n$ / rpm & $T_k$ & $\Delta n$ ($\Delta=\SI{4}{\micro s}$) & $\Delta n$ ($\Delta=\SI{0.5}{\micro s}$) & ripple ($\pm1\,\%$) \\
\midrule
600  & \SI{1667}{\micro s} & \SI{1.4}{rpm} & \SI{0.2}{rpm} & $\pm$\SI{6}{rpm} \\
3000 & \SI{333}{\micro s}  & \SI{36}{rpm}  & \SI{4.5}{rpm} & $\pm$\SI{30}{rpm} \\
7000 & \SI{143}{\micro s}  & \SI{196}{rpm} & \SI{24.5}{rpm}& $\pm$\SI{70}{rpm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The relative resolution $\Delta n/n=\Delta/T_k=n\Delta$ gets worse linearly
with speed. The quantisation step equals the ripple amplitude when
$n^2\Delta=0.01\,n$, i.e.\ at $n=0.01/\Delta$: \SI{2500}{rpm} for
\texttt{micros()}, \SI{20000}{rpm} for Timer1. Above \SI{2500}{rpm} the ripple
is therefore buried in the \texttt{micros()} quantisation. With the
\SI{0.5}{\micro s} timer it is resolved over the whole speed range. Real ECUs
use capture timers with $\le\SI{100}{ns}$ resolution.

Step 4, robustness of the gap test. Let $\omega_1$ be the speed during the
tooth before the gap and $\omega_2$ the mean speed across the gap. Then
\[ \frac{T_{\text{gap}}}{T_{\text{prev}}}=\frac{18^\circ/\omega_2}{6^\circ/\omega_1}
   =3\,\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2}. \]
\emph{Missed gap:} the ratio drops to 2 or less if $\omega_2\ge1.5\,\omega_1$.
The centres of the two intervals are $3^\circ+9^\circ=12^\circ$ apart, i.e.\
$\Delta t\approx12^\circ/\omega_1$. The speed must therefore rise by
$0.5\,\omega_1$ within $\Delta t$:
\[ \dot\omega\approx\frac{0.5\,\omega_1}{12^\circ/\omega_1}=\frac{\omega_1^2}{24^\circ}. \]
Convert to rpm with $\omega=6n$ ($\omega$ in $^\circ$/s, $n$ in rpm):
$6\dot n=36n^2/24$, so $\dot n\approx n^2/4$ (rpm/s). At \SI{600}{rpm} this is
$600^2/4=\SI{90000}{rpm/s}$.

\emph{False gap:} a normal tooth gives a ratio $T_k/T_{k-1}>2$ only if the
speed halves within one tooth ($\Delta t\approx6^\circ/\omega$):
$\dot\omega\approx-0.5\,\omega^2/6^\circ$, i.e.\ $\dot n\approx-n^2/2$, which
is \SI{-180000}{rpm/s} at \SI{600}{rpm}.

Both limits are an order of magnitude beyond real engines
($\lesssim\SI{10000}{rpm/s}$). The offline model confirms the large margin:
at 3000 and \SI{7000}{rpm} the measured gap ratio lies in $2.97\dots3.09$ and
the ratio of normal teeth stays below $1.03$. So threshold 2 is robust in
normal operation (the geometric mean of 1 and 3 would be 1.73). Only the start
is critical: when cranking at \SI{200}{rpm}, the compression strokes cause
$\pm30\,\%$ speed fluctuations, and the limit is
$\dot n\approx200^2/4=\SI{10000}{rpm/s}$. That is why ECUs add plausibility
checks there (tooth count 58 between gaps, cam edge position).

\textbf{b) ISR.} The tooth numbers follow from $\theta=6^\circ k$:
$k=120/6=20$ and $k=300/6=50$. The LED stays on for $30^\circ/6^\circ=5$ teeth.
The reference solution
(\codefile{solutions/wokwi/sheet01\_crank/sketch.ino}, line numbers as in the
file) works in three steps.

\emph{Step 1 -- time stamp and tooth period.} Unsigned subtraction gives the
right period even when the counter wraps around. The 16-bit Timer1 wraps every
$65536\cdot\SI{0.5}{\micro s}=\SI{32.8}{ms}$, so periods up to \SI{32.8}{ms}
are measured correctly. The longest interval is the gap, which limits the
method to $n>3/\SI{32.8}{ms}\approx\SI{92}{rpm}$; that is enough for cranking.
The first edge only initialises \texttt{t\_last}:
\inputcode[firstline=52,lastline=60,firstnumber=52]{solutions/wokwi/sheet01_crank/sketch.ino}
Note \texttt{T\_prev != 0}: right after the start there is no previous period,
so no gap decision is made (this is the extra edge needed in 1.1\,d).

\emph{Step 2 -- gap and tooth counter.} At the gap the counter is reset to 0
and the accumulated revolution statistics go to \texttt{loop()}. If the
previous revolution did not end at tooth 57, an edge was lost or counted twice,
and \texttt{sync\_errors} is incremented. Every other edge increments
\texttt{tooth} and updates sum, minimum and maximum of $T$:
\inputcode[firstline=62,lastline=81,firstnumber=62]{solutions/wokwi/sheet01_crank/sketch.ino}

\emph{Step 3 -- TDC LED.} Switch it on at teeth 20/50 and off 5 teeth later:

Expected result: every revolution reports \texttt{teeth} $=58$ edges (57
normal teeth + the gap edge) and \texttt{sync\_err} $=0$. In the logic
analyzer the LED (D3) rises a few microseconds (interrupt latency) after the
rising edge of tooth 20 (50) on D0. This is practically together with the
\SI{50}{\micro s} TDC pulse of the chip on D2, which is emitted exactly at
$120^\circ$/$300^\circ$. At \SI{3000}{rpm} the LED is lit for
$5\cdot\SI{333}{\micro s}=\SI{1.67}{ms}$ every \SI{10}{ms}. The LED marks the
crank-angle \emph{event}.

\textbf{c) Speed computation.} \texttt{loop()} copies the shared variables
with interrupts disabled. Without that, the ISR could change a multi-byte
value halfway through the read. It then applies \eqref{eq:rpm}.
The \emph{longest} tooth period gives the \emph{lowest} speed, so
\texttt{rpm\_min} uses \texttt{tmax}:
\inputcode[firstline=139,lastline=151,firstnumber=139]{solutions/wokwi/sheet01_crank/sketch.ino}

\emph{Step 1 -- per-revolution resolution.} One revolution lasts
$\SI{20}{ms}=5000$ ticks (\texttt{micros()}) or $40000$ ticks (Timer1). One
tick therefore gives $\Delta n=3000/5000=\SI{0.6}{rpm}$, resp.\
$3000/40000=\SI{0.075}{rpm}$.

\emph{Step 2 -- expected per-tooth values.} With the ripple, the tooth period
varies by $\pm1\,\%$, i.e.\ $T_k=330.0\dots\SI{336.7}{\micro s}$. Since
\texttt{micros()} returns multiples of \SI{4}{\micro s}, the measured period is
one of $328, 332, 336, \SI{340}{\micro s}$, which gives the speed levels
$3048.8, 3012.0, 2976.2, \SI{2941.2}{rpm}$. These levels are
$\approx\SI{36}{rpm}$ apart, as predicted in a).

\emph{Step 3 -- check with the offline model.}
\codefile{solutions/wokwi/sheet01\_crank/tooth\_sim.c} reproduces exactly this
measurement over 200 revolutions:

  per-revolution rpm 2999.40..3000.00
$ ./tooth_sim 3000 0.5
rpm 3000, tick 0.50 us: per-tooth rpm mean 3000.6 std 21.7 min 2967.4 max 3030.3 (11286 teeth)
  per-revolution rpm 2999.85..2999.85
\end{lstlisting}
(In Wokwi, rare extra outliers of a few \si{\micro s} can occur when the crank
interrupt has to wait for the Timer0 or UART interrupt.)
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}

\midrule

\emph{Step 4 -- interpretation.} The per-revolution speed is slightly
\emph{below} 3000. The reason is that the revolution time is
$T_{\text{rev}}=\int_0^{360^\circ}\dd\theta/\omega$, and the mean of
$1/(1+\varepsilon\sin2\theta)$ is $\approx1+\varepsilon^2/2$. So
$n_{\text{rev}}\approx n(1-\varepsilon^2/2)=3000-\SI{0.15}{rpm}$.

The per-tooth spread splits into two independent parts, which add as
variances:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \emph{Information}: the real ripple, a sine of amplitude
    \SI{30}{rpm} with 2 periods per revolution. Its std is
    $30/\sqrt2=\SI{21.2}{rpm}$. On a real engine these are the torque pulses
    of the individual combustions, used e.g.\ for misfire detection.
  \item \emph{Measurement error}: each period is the difference of two time
    stamps, and each stamp has a rounding error that is uniform over one tick.
    The difference therefore has variance $2\cdot\Delta^2/12=\Delta^2/6$, i.e.\
    a std of $n^2\Delta/\sqrt6$: \SI{14.7}{rpm} for \texttt{micros()}, \SI{1.8}{rpm}
    for Timer1.
\end{itemize}
\[ \sigma_{\text{micros}}\approx\sqrt{21.2^2+14.7^2}=\SI{25.8}{rpm},\qquad
   \sigma_{\text{Timer1}}\approx\sqrt{21.2^2+1.8^2}=\SI{21.3}{rpm}, \]
in good agreement with 25.7 and \SI{21.7}{rpm}. With Timer1 almost all of the
``jitter'' is information. With \texttt{micros()}, the measurement error is of
the same size as the information. The plotted dump shows a clean sine with 2
periods per 58 teeth for Timer1, and a staircase-like, noisy version for
\texttt{micros()}. The gap lines (\texttt{tooth} = 255) are already
converted with $3/T$ in the dump, so they do not show up as outliers.

\textbf{d) \SI{7000}{rpm}.} Use the same steps with $T_k=\SI{142.9}{\micro s}$.
\texttt{micros()} gives periods $140\dots\SI{148}{\micro s}$, i.e.\
$7142.9\dots\SI{6756.8}{rpm}$. The predicted std is
$\sqrt{(70/\sqrt2)^2+(196/\sqrt6)^2}=\sqrt{49.5^2+80.0^2}=\SI{94}{rpm}$
for \texttt{micros()} and $\sqrt{49.5^2+10.0^2}=\SI{50.5}{rpm}$ for Timer1. The model gives:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=shell,numbers=none]
$ ./tooth_sim 7000 4
rpm 7000, tick 4.00 us: per-tooth rpm mean 7002.1 std 94.2 min 6756.8 max 7142.9 (11286 teeth)
  per-revolution rpm 6999.53..7002.80
$ ./tooth_sim 7000 0.5
rpm 7000, tick 0.50 us: per-tooth rpm mean 7001.2 std 51.3 min 6920.4 max 7092.2 (11286 teeth)
  per-revolution rpm 6999.53..6999.94
\end{lstlisting}
With \texttt{micros()} the measurement error is now larger than the ripple
($\pm\SI{70}{rpm}$). The per-revolution value stays within $\pm\SI{3}{rpm}$
(one tick out of $\SI{8.57}{ms}/\SI{4}{\micro s}=2143$ ticks) resp.\
\SI{0.5}{rpm}. When the slider is moved, the speed changes abruptly in the
simulator. The gap is still found (ratio 3 vs.\ 1, see a)) as long as the jump
does not fall exactly into the gap.

\emph{UART load:}
\begin{align*}
  \text{edges/s} &= \frac{7000}{60}\cdot58 \approx 6770\ \text{s}^{-1},\\
  \text{chars/s} &\approx 6770\cdot20 \approx 135\,000\ \text{s}^{-1}
    \quad(\text{line \texttt{1234,57,286,6993.0} + CR/LF}),\\
  \text{UART capacity} &= \frac{\SI{115200}{bit/s}}{10\ \text{bit/char}}
    = 11\,520\ \text{chars/s}.
\end{align*}
Printing one line per tooth would need almost $12\times$ the UART capacity.
One line per revolution ($\approx50$ chars, $117$ rev/s) needs
$\approx5800$ chars/s, i.e.\ half the capacity. Hence statistics per
revolution, and a block dump from RAM that is printed after the recording
(120 lines of $\approx16$ chars take $\approx\SI{170}{ms}$).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}{Cam/crank synchronisation}{\Challenge}
Extend your sketch such that it determines the $720^\circ$ phase from the cam
signal (\texttt{eng:CAM}, HIGH for $0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ$).
\begin{tasks}
  \item Why should the cam level not be read at the reference edge
    ($\theta=0^\circ$), and which tooth is a good choice instead?
  \item Implement \texttt{phase} ($0^\circ$ or $360^\circ$), toggle it at every
    gap, and check it at every revolution (\texttt{cam\_err}). Light the LED on
    pin~11 only at the compression TDC of cylinder~1 and verify it with the
    logic analyzer (D1 = cam, D4 = LED).
  \item Give the crank angle and the number of the cylinder that fires at each of
    the four TDC events.
\end{tasks}
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{a) Where to read the cam level.} The cam edge at $\theta=0^\circ$
coincides with the reference edge of the crank signal. In the simulator both
are written in the same event. On a real engine the cam edge has a tolerance
of several degrees and drifts with cam phasing. Reading the level at the
reference edge is therefore a race: the result depends on which edge comes
first. The level must be read in the \emph{middle} of a cam state, far from
both cam edges ($0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$), e.g.\ at tooth~10 ($60^\circ$ or
$420^\circ$):
\[ \text{HIGH at tooth 10}\Rightarrow\theta=60^\circ\ (\text{phase }0^\circ),
   \qquad
   \text{LOW at tooth 10}\Rightarrow\theta=420^\circ\ (\text{phase }360^\circ). \]
Any tooth whose distance to $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ is $\ge10^\circ$ works,
i.e.\ teeth $2\dots28$.

\textbf{b) Implementation.} The phase logic consists of two parts.
Step 1: \emph{predict}. Every gap starts a new revolution, so the phase
toggles between 0 and $360^\circ$ (inside the gap branch of the ISR):
\inputcode[firstline=72,lastline=72,firstnumber=72]{solutions/wokwi/sheet01_crank/sketch.ino}
Step 2: \emph{measure and check}. At tooth 10 the cam level gives the phase
directly. If a prediction exists and the two disagree, \texttt{cam\_err} is
incremented. The measured value always wins, which also does the first
synchronisation (\texttt{phase} $=-1$ before):
\inputcode[firstline=94,lastline=99,firstnumber=94]{solutions/wokwi/sheet01_crank/sketch.ino}
Step 3: \emph{use it}. The cylinder-1 LED is switched together with the TDC
LED, but only at tooth 20 \emph{and} phase $0^\circ$, i.e.\ at $\theta=120^\circ$
(line 87 in the listing of Exercise~1.2\,b).

Expected result: \texttt{cam\_err} stays 0 and the printed \texttt{phase}
alternates 0, 360, 0, \dots{} The green LED (D4) is on only every second time
the red TDC LED lights up at tooth 20, i.e.\ once per engine cycle (every
\SI{40}{ms} at \SI{3000}{rpm}). It always lies inside the cam HIGH phase on
D1 (at $120^\circ$, cam HIGH for $0\dots180^\circ$).

\textbf{c) Cylinder assignment.} The crank angle of an edge is
$\theta=\text{phase}+6^\circ k$. Insert the two TDC teeth and the two phases,
then use the TDC positions of the model (cylinders 1, 3, 4, 2 at 120, 300,
480, $660^\circ$, firing order 1-3-4-2):
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
\toprule
phase & tooth & crank angle & cylinder \\
\midrule
$0^\circ$   & 20 & $0+6\cdot20=120^\circ$ & 1 \\
$0^\circ$   & 50 & $0+6\cdot50=300^\circ$ & 3 \\
$360^\circ$ & 20 & $360+120=480^\circ$ & 4 \\

\lstinline!cyl[phase == 360][tooth == 50]!.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}{Signal zoo}{\JSLinux}
The program \codefile{bonus/jslinux/sheet01/signal\_zoo.c} generates \SI{160}{ms}
(four engine cycles) of the crank, cam, knock and ion signals at
\SI{3000}{rpm}, $f_s=\SI{25}{kHz}$, and \SI{20}{s} of a lambda sensor signal
($f_s=\SI{200}{Hz}$) for a slowly oscillating $\lambda(t)$. Additionally, a crank
signal during a speed ramp \SIrange{2000}{4000}{rpm} is generated.
\begin{tasks}
  \item Complete \texttt{stats()}: mean, RMS value, minimum and maximum. Run
    the program and explain the values of the crank and cam signals
    analytically. Why is the mean of the knock signal not zero, although the
    knock sensor is AC coupled?
  \item The normalised autocorrelation
    \[ r[m] = \frac{\hat R[m]}{\hat R[0]},\qquad
       \hat R[m]=\frac{1}{N-m}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-m}(x[n]-\bar x)(x[n+m]-\bar x) \]
    is close to 1 at lags $m$ where the signal resembles a shifted copy of
    itself. Complete \texttt{autocorr()} and \texttt{period\_lag()} (first local
    maximum after the first zero crossing that reaches at least 90\,\% of the
    largest value). Report the estimated period and $r$ for all six signals.
  \item Interpret the results: Which signals are periodic, which only
    quasi-periodic? Why does the crank signal show a strong peak already at
    half a revolution? What happens to the periodicity of the crank signal
    during the ramp -- and which lag is still detected? Why would the
    $1/N$-normalised (biased) estimate be misleading here?
\end{tasks}
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{a) Statistics.} \texttt{stats()} needs only one loop over the
samples. It accumulates $\sum x$ and $\sum x^2$ and tracks the extremes. Then
$\bar x=\frac1N\sum x[n]$ and
$x_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum x[n]^2}$ (reference solution
\codefile{solutions/bonus/jslinux/sheet01/signal\_zoo.c}):
\inputcode[firstline=30,lastline=43,firstnumber=30]{solutions/bonus/jslinux/sheet01/signal_zoo.c}
Output:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=shell,numbers=none]
signal              mean       rms       min       max
crank             0.4808    0.6934    0.0000    1.0000
cam               0.2500    0.5000    0.0000    1.0000
knock             0.0433    0.1234   -1.1454    1.5144
ion               0.0993    0.2399   -0.1269    1.0440
lambda            0.5004    0.6195    0.1000    0.9000
\end{lstlisting}
\emph{Binary signals, analytically.} Let $x\in\{0,1\}$ with duty cycle $d$
(fraction of samples that are 1). Then $\bar x=d$. Because $x^2=x$, also
$\overline{x^2}=d$, so $x_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt d$.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item Crank: 58 teeth with $3^\circ$ HIGH each per $360^\circ$, so
    $d=58\cdot3/360=0.4833$. Measured: 0.4808 and
    $\sqrt{0.4808}=0.6934$. The small deviation (0.0025, i.e.\ about 10 of
    4000 samples) is a sampling effect. One sample is
    $\SI{18000}{\degree/s}/\SI{25}{kHz}=0.72^\circ$, so a HIGH phase of
    $3^\circ$ lasts only 4.17 sampling intervals and is represented by 4 or 5
    samples, depending on where the sampling grid lies. Over the short record
    these roundings do not average out exactly.
  \item Cam: $d=180/720=0.25$, RMS $=\sqrt{0.25}=0.5$ -- exact.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Knock mean.} The knock signal contains a low-frequency combustion
vibration: a positive half sine of amplitude $A=\SI{0.2}{V}$ during $60^\circ$
after every TDC, i.e.\ during $60^\circ$ of every $180^\circ$. The mean of a
half sine over its own duration is $2A/\pi$, so
\[ \bar x = A\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{60^\circ}{180^\circ}
   = 0.2\cdot0.637\cdot0.333 = \SI{0.042}{V}, \]
which is the measured mean. (The noise, the valve bursts and the knock
oscillations have zero mean.) A real piezo sensor is AC-coupled. The model
represents the signal after a high-pass whose cut-off is far below the
combustion frequency, so this component survives. The large extremes
($-1.15/+1.51$\,V) come from knock events.

\emph{Lambda RMS.} The mean of 0.50 matches a signal that is lean and rich
half of the time each. A pure two-level signal (0.1/0.9\,V) would have
RMS $\sqrt{0.5\cdot0.1^2+0.5\cdot0.9^2}=0.640$. The measured value is
lower (0.6195) because the sensor curve
$V=0.5+0.4\tanh\bigl((1-\lambda)/0.005\bigr)$ with
$\lambda=1+0.02\sin\varphi$ spends some time in the transition region:
$\overline{V^2}=0.25+0.16\,\overline{\tanh^2(4\sin\varphi)}=0.25+0.16\cdot0.836=0.384$,
so $\sqrt{0.384}=0.6195$.

\textbf{b) Autocorrelation and period search.}
\emph{Small example by hand:} $x=[1,1,0,0,1,1,0,0]$ (period 4), $\bar x=0.5$,
deviations $\pm0.5$. At lag 2 every product is $(+0.5)(-0.5)$, at lag 4 every
product is $(\pm0.5)^2$, so $\hat R[0]=0.25$, $\hat R[2]=-0.25$,
$\hat R[4]=0.25$, and $r[2]=-1$, $r[4]=1$. The division by $N-m$ (not $N$) is
what makes $r[4]$ exactly 1, although only 4 products exist at that lag.

\texttt{autocorr()} implements the formula literally. First it removes the
mean, then it averages $N-m$ products per lag and normalises by $\hat R[0]$:
\inputcode[firstline=48,lastline=61,firstnumber=48]{solutions/bonus/jslinux/sheet01/signal_zoo.c}
\texttt{period\_lag()} takes three steps. (1) It skips the main lobe around
$m=0$ (every signal resembles itself for small shifts) up to the first zero
crossing. (2) It finds the largest value $r_{\max}$ beyond. (3) It returns the
\emph{first} local maximum with $r\ge0.9\,r_{\max}$. Taking the first one
avoids picking a multiple of the period, whose peak is almost as high:
\inputcode[firstline=67,lastline=80,firstnumber=67]{solutions/bonus/jslinux/sheet01/signal_zoo.c}
Result:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=shell,numbers=none]
crank 3000   first peak: lag  250 =   10.00 ms r = 0.929 | max: lag  500 r = 0.988
crank ramp   first peak: lag    8 =    0.32 ms r = 0.348 | max: lag    8 r = 0.348
cam          first peak: lag 1000 =   40.00 ms r = 1.000 | max: lag 1000 r = 1.000
knock        first peak: lag  244 =    9.76 ms r = 0.418 | max: lag  500 r = 0.438
ion          first peak: lag  250 =   10.00 ms r = 0.992 | max: lag  250 r = 0.992
lambda       first peak: lag  251 = 1255.00 ms r = 0.981 | max: lag  500 r = 0.982
\end{lstlisting}
Expected lags at $f_s=\SI{25}{kHz}$: $180^\circ=\SI{10}{ms}=250$ samples,
revolution $=500$, engine cycle $=1000$. For lambda at \SI{200}{Hz}:
$1/\SI{0.8}{Hz}=\SI{1.25}{s}=250$ samples.

\textbf{c) Interpretation.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Cam}: exactly periodic, $r=1.000$ at \SI{40}{ms} = one engine cycle.
  \item \emph{Crank}: the true period is one revolution (\SI{20}{ms}, $r=0.988$).
    It is not exactly 1 because of the speed ripple and because the period is
    500.03 samples, not an integer. Why is $r(250)$ almost as high? Shift the
    wheel by $180^\circ$: all teeth fall onto teeth, except that the two missing teeth
    (58, 59 at $348^\circ$, $354^\circ$) meet the real teeth 28, 29 at
    $168^\circ$, $174^\circ$, and vice versa. So $x$ and its shifted copy $y$ differ on
    $2\cdot2\cdot3^\circ=12^\circ$ of $360^\circ$. With
    $\overline{(x-y)^2}=12/360=0.033$ and
    $\overline{xy}-\bar x^2=\sigma^2-\frac12\overline{(x-y)^2}$, where
    $\sigma^2=d(1-d)=0.250$:
    \[ r(250)\approx1-\frac{0.033/2}{0.250}=0.933 \quad(\text{measured }0.929). \]
    The gap, which carries the position information, is only a small part of
    the signal energy. With the 90\,\% rule the detector therefore reports
    $180^\circ$, a reminder that a period detector must be designed for the
    feature of interest. The peaks are very narrow: one sample less
    ($0.72^\circ$) shifts every tooth edge and gives $r(249)=0.48$. (Small
    peaks at multiples of 8.33 samples come from the teeth.)
  \item \emph{Crank during the ramp}: the tooth period $6^\circ/\omega$
    shrinks from \SI{0.5}{ms} (\SI{2000}{rpm}) to \SI{0.25}{ms}
    (\SI{4000}{rpm}), i.e.\ from 12.5 to 6.25 samples (mean $\approx8.3$). No long-lag peak survives, and only the tooth-scale
    peak at lag 8 ($r=0.35$) remains. In time the signal is not periodic. In
    the angle domain it would be (Sheet~4).
  \item \emph{Knock}: $r\approx0.42$ at $\approx\SI{10}{ms}$ ($180^\circ$).
    Only the deterministic parts (combustion vibration, valve impacts at
    $8$\,kHz) repeat. Noise and the random knock bursts (random onset and
    phase) do not correlate. So the signal is quasi-periodic, i.e.\
    cyclostationary. The lag 244 instead of 250 is a side effect of the
    \SI{8}{kHz} valve bursts: their period is only 3.1 samples, so $r[m]$
    jumps between 0.30 and 0.44 from lag to lag around $m=250$. The first
    local maximum above the threshold is then at 244.
  \item \emph{Ion current}: $r=0.992$ at $180^\circ$. In the model all
    cylinders produce the same deterministic ion curve. On a real engine
    cycle-to-cycle variations make each event an individual (aperiodic)
    transient.
  \item \emph{Lambda}: period \SI{1.25}{s} = $1/\SI{0.8}{Hz}$, $r=0.98$. The
    switching sensor turns the small $\lambda$ oscillation into an almost
    rectangular signal. Noise shifts the switching instants slightly, hence
    251 instead of 250.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Biased estimator.} With $1/N$ instead of $1/(N-m)$, every $r[m]$ is
multiplied by $(N-m)/N$. At lag 1000 of a 4000-sample record this is a factor
of $3000/4000=0.75$, so the cam signal would show $r=0.75$ although it is
perfectly periodic. The factor also decreases with $m$, so shorter lags would
always be favoured: the crank peak at lag 500 would drop to $0.988\cdot0.875=0.86$.
\end{solution}
