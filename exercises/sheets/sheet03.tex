\sheet{3}{LTI Systems}{Chapter~3 (Linear Time-Invariant Systems)}

\begin{goals}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Compute convolutions in C -- as a table of products and as an
    efficient routine -- and verify the algebraic properties of convolution
    numerically.
  \item Obtain impulse and step responses of the two workhorse smoothers --
    moving average (FIR) and exponential smoother (IIR) -- by iterating their
    difference equations, and identify the closed forms.
  \item Quantify the noise-versus-lag trade-off of a smoother (noise gain,
    mean delay, ramp lag) numerically and relate it to the step response.
  \item Measure the frequency response of an LTI system with a complex
    exponential input (eigenfunction).
  \item Apply LTI filters to the per-tooth engine speed in real time and
    understand why the missing-tooth gap must be handled \emph{before} the
    filter (time invariance in the tooth domain).
\end{itemize}
\end{goals}

\section*{Background}
\emph{Interactive companion page (in German):}
\href{https://claude.ai/artifact/EAoZ2MJx2TeUcwUd38kTHN}{\texttt{claude.ai/artifact/EAoZ2MJx2TeUcwUd38kTHN}}
-- impulse response, Fourier transform, discrete and continuous convolution,
convolution theorem and measuring an impulse response, with sliders. The same
page is in the repository as
\texttt{slides/\allowbreak convolution/\allowbreak Faltung\_LTI.html}
(download it and open it in any browser).

A discrete-time LTI system is completely described by its impulse response
$h[n]=S\{\delta[n]\}$; the output for any input is the convolution
\[
  y[n]=(x*h)[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k]\,h[n-k] .
\]
Two smoothers are used throughout the course (written for the per-tooth speed
$r[k]$, but valid for any sequence):
\begin{align*}
  \text{MA (FIR):}\quad & y[n]=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x[n-k]
     = y[n-1]+\frac{x[n]-x[n-N]}{N},\\
  \text{EMA (IIR):}\quad & y[n]=a\,y[n-1]+(1-a)\,x[n]
     = y[n-1]+\alpha\,(x[n]-y[n-1]),\qquad \alpha=1-a,
\end{align*}
called moving average and exponential smoother (exponential moving average).
Useful characteristics of a smoother with $\sum_n h[n]=1$: the \emph{noise gain}
$\sum_n h^2[n]$ (variance reduction for white noise) and the \emph{mean delay}
$D=\sum_n n\,h[n]$. The step response is $s[n]=\sum_{k\le n}h[k]$, the
frequency response $H(\Omega)=\sum_k h[k]\,\e^{-\jj\Omega k}$.

\emph{Reading the table.} Row $k$ is the impulse response $h$ scaled by $x[k]$
and shifted to start at $n=k$ -- the response of the system to the single
input sample $x[k]\,\delta[n-k]$. The output is the superposition of these
rows (linearity + time invariance). Column $n$ contains exactly the products
of the graphical method: $h[n-k]$ as a function of $k$ is $h$ flipped and
moved to position $n$; multiplying with $x[k]$ and summing over the overlap
gives $y[n]$. Length $n_x+n_h-1=4+3-1=6$.

\emph{Sum check.} Summing the whole table row by row gives
$\sum_n y[n]=\sum_k x[k]\sum_n h[n-k]=\sum_k x[k]\cdot\sum_m h[m]$ (substitute
$m=n-k$): every row sums to $x[k]\sum h$. Here $1=2\cdot0.5$. In frequency
terms this is $Y(0)=X(0)\,H(0)$: the DC gain of a cascade is the product of
the DC gains. The check is cheap but weak -- it catches a missing or doubled
term, not a term in the wrong column.

\emph{Closed forms.} MA: the impulse enters the window at $n=0$ and leaves it
after $N$ samples, so $h[n]=1/N$ for $0\le n\le N-1$ and 0 otherwise
($0.0625=1/16$ for $n=0\dots15$). The step response is the running sum,
$s[n]=(n+1)/N$ for $0\le n<N$ and $s[n]=1$ for $n\ge N-1$: a linear ramp that
is \emph{finished} after $N$ samples. EMA: iterating
$y[n]=a\,y[n-1]+(1-a)\,\delta[n]$ from rest gives $y[0]=1-a$, $y[1]=a(1-a)$,
$y[2]=a^2(1-a)$, \dots, i.e.\ $h[n]=(1-a)\,a^n\,u[n]$ (the table shows the
constant ratio $h[n+1]/h[n]=0.88235=15/17$, and $h[0]=2/17=0.11765$). Its step
response is the geometric sum $s[n]=(1-a)\sum_{k=0}^{n}a^k=1-a^{n+1}$: it
approaches 1 exponentially and never reaches it. The deviation of

\emph{Noise gain.} For white noise of variance $\sigma^2$ the output variance is
$\sigma^2\sum h^2$ (the samples are uncorrelated, so the variances of the
weighted terms add). MA: $\sum h^2=N\cdot1/N^2=1/N=0.0625$. EMA:
$\sum h^2=(1-a)^2\sum a^{2n}=(1-a)^2/(1-a^2)=(1-a)/(1+a)=(2/17)/(32/17)=0.0625$.
The measured variance ratios (19\,500 samples) agree within the statistical
uncertainty of about 2\,\%.

\emph{Mean delay.} MA: $D=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}n=\frac{N-1}{2}=7.5$. EMA:
$D=(1-a)\sum n\,a^n=(1-a)\frac{a}{(1-a)^2}=\frac{a}{1-a}=\frac{15/17}{2/17}=7.5$.

\emph{Why the three lag numbers agree.} Ramp: for $n$ beyond the length of $h$
(for the EMA asymptotically)
$y[n]=\sum_k h[k]\,c\,(n-k)=c\,n\sum_k h[k]-c\sum_k k\,h[k]=c\,(n-D)$ -- the output
is the input ramp shifted by $D$ samples; the program finds $n-y[n]=7.500$ at
$n=400$. Step response: $1-s[n]=\sum_{k>n}h[k]$, hence
$\sum_{n\ge0}(1-s[n])=\sum_{n\ge0}\sum_{k>n}h[k]=\sum_k h[k]\cdot\#\{n:0\le n<k\}
=\sum_k k\,h[k]=D$. The ramp lag can thus be read off the step response as the
area between the step and the response. (By linearity the ramp response is the
running sum of the step response, which is the same statement.)

\emph{Equal noise gain.} The bisection converges to
$a=0.6, 0.7778, 0.88235, 0.93939$, which are exactly $(N-1)/(N+1)=3/5, 7/9, 15/17,
31/33$ -- from $(1-a)/(1+a)=1/N$ one gets $a=(N-1)/(N+1)$, i.e.\
$\alpha=2/(N+1)$ (the usual ``$N$-period EMA''). With this $a$ the mean delay is
$D_{\text{EMA}}=a/(1-a)=(N-1)/2=D_{\text{MA}}$ -- in every row of the table:
\emph{for equal white-noise reduction both smoothers have exactly the same
ramp lag.} They differ in the shape of the step response (b) and in how they
suppress periodic disturbances (d).

coefficients, so by linearity
$S\{\cos\Omega n+\jj\sin\Omega n\}=S\{\cos\Omega n\}+\jj\,S\{\sin\Omega n\}$.

\emph{Eigenfunction:}
$y[n]=\sum_k h[k]\,\e^{\jj\Omega(n-k)}=\e^{\jj\Omega n}\sum_k h[k]\,\e^{-\jj\Omega k}
=H(\Omega)\,x[n]$ -- the input comes out unchanged up to the complex factor
$H(\Omega)$, which does not depend on $n$. The program shows this: after the
start-up ($n\ge300$) the ratio $y[n]/x[n]$ is the same number for every $n$ and
equals the DTFT sum. (Before the start-up has decayed the ratio is not constant:
the eigenfunction property holds for an input that started at $n=-\infty$.)

\emph{Closed forms as a check.} MA: geometric sum,
$H=\frac{1}{N}\frac{1-\e^{-\jj\Omega N}}{1-\e^{-\jj\Omega}}
=\e^{-\jj\Omega(N-1)/2}\,\frac{\sin(N\Omega/2)}{N\sin(\Omega/2)}$. MA$_{16}$:
$\frac{\sin(16\pi/30)}{16\sin(\pi/30)}=\frac{0.9945}{1.6724}=0.5946$, with the
linear phase $-\Omega\cdot7.5=-\pi/2$ -- hence the purely imaginary $-0.5946\jj$
(phase delay = mean delay = 7.5 samples). EMA:
$H=\frac{1-a}{1-a\e^{-\jj\Omega}}$, $|H|=0.1176/0.2289=0.5139$, phase

\emph{Zeros of MA$_{30}$.} $|H_{\text{MA}N}|=0$ whenever $N\Omega/2=m\pi$, i.e.\ at
$\Omega=2\pi m/N$. For $N=30$ and $\Omega=2\pi\cdot\text{order}/60$ these are the
even orders 2, 4, 6, \dots: the window spans $30\cdot6^\circ=180^\circ$, an
integer number of periods of every even order, and the sum of a sinusoid over
whole periods vanishes. The 2nd-order speed ripple (one period per combustion,
4 cylinders) is removed \emph{completely}; MA$_{16}$ (96$^\circ$) only damps it
to 59\,\%, the EMA with the same noise gain to 51\,\%. These factors are
confirmed by the simulation in Exercise~3.3\,a).

proves nothing about rounding. (c): The truncated impulse response misses the
tail $\sum_{n\ge L}h[n]=a^L$; for an input of magnitude $\approx1.1$ (step + noise)
the error is $\approx1.1\,a^L$, as observed. The recursion needs 2
multiplications per sample for an \emph{infinite} impulse response, the
convolution $L$ -- the reason why IIR filters are cheap. The recursive step
response matches $1-a^{n+1}$ to rounding precision.

convolution, like the means of independent random variables). The step response
of the cascade is a smooth S-curve (piecewise parabolic) reaching 1 after 15
samples instead of the linear ramp of one MA reaching 1 after 8 samples:
better high-frequency suppression, twice the delay.

\emph{MA in the tooth domain: impossible.} At \SI{1000}{rpm} one sample takes
\SI{1}{ms}; \SI{5000}{rpm/s} are 5\,rpm per sample, and 0.5\,\% are 5\,rpm, so
the lag may be at most one sample. The lag of MA$_N$ is $N/2$ samples
($(N-1)/2+0.5$, see a): the mean error grows as $-0.25\,\%\cdot N$ (MA$_8$:
$-2.0\,\%$, MA$_{16}$: $-4.1\,\%$). Even the raw speed (MA$_1$, lag half a sample,
$-0.22\,\%$) fails, because it contains the full ripple of $\pm1\,\%$; MA$_2$
still passes the ripple almost unchanged ($|H|=\cos(\pi/30)=0.995$). The
attenuation of the 2nd order, $|H|=\sin(N\pi/30)/(N\sin(\pi/30))$, falls only
slowly with $N$ (0.97, 0.89, 0.59 for $N=4,8,16$) and reaches 0 at $N=30$. To
bring the ripple below 0.25\,\% an MA needs $|H|\le0.25$, i.e.\ $N\ge24$
($|H|=0.23$), which lags 12 samples $=6\,\%$; MA$_{30}$ removes the ripple but
lags 8\,\%. The EMA with the same noise gain ($\alpha=2/31$) has
the same lag and additionally passes 51\,\% of the ripple. A causal smoother
in the tooth domain cannot have both small lag and ripple suppression.

\emph{LTI lag compensation over-corrects.} $y_c[k]=y[k]+\frac N2\cdot
\frac{y[k]-y[k-N]}{N}=1.5\,y[k]-0.5\,y[k-30]$ is still an FIR filter with the
zeros of MA$_{30}$ at the even orders, and for a ramp that is linear in the
\emph{sample index} it removes the lag exactly (gain $1.5-0.5=1$, total lag including the half sample of the tooth
measurement $1.5\cdot15-0.5\cdot45=0$). But the engine accelerates linearly in \emph{time}:
per tooth the speed rises by $\SI{5000}{rpm/s}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\,\mathrm{s}
=5000/n$ rpm, i.e.\ 5.6\,rpm at 900\,rpm but 5.0\,rpm at 1000\,rpm. The slope
estimate $(y[k]-y[k-30])/30$ describes the speed 15--45 teeth ago, when it rose
faster, so the extrapolation shoots over by $\approx\SI{12}{rpm}$ ($+1.2\,\%$).
In the tooth domain (uniform in angle) a ramp in time is not a ramp.

\emph{Segment time + prediction works.} The segment speed
$n_{\text{seg}}=30/T_{\text{seg}}$ is the \emph{time} average of the speed over
the last $180^\circ$: $T_{\text{seg}}=\int\!\dd t$ and $\int n\,\dd t\propto180^\circ$.
For a speed linear in time this is exactly the speed at the middle of the
segment, $T_{\text{seg}}/2$ ago (mean of a linear function over an interval);
its error ($-8.29\,\%$) is pure lag, and because the segment spans one full
period of the 2nd order, the ripple is removed (it enters $T_{\text{seg}}$ only
quadratically, $\approx\varepsilon^2/2=5\cdot10^{-5}$). Two adjacent segment
speeds give the acceleration exactly, $\dot n=(n_{\text{seg}}[k]-n_{\text{seg}}[k-30])/
\bigl(\tfrac12(T_{\text{seg}}[k]+T_{\text{seg}}[k-30])\bigr)$, and
$\hat n=n_{\text{seg}}+\dot n\,T_{\text{seg}}/2$ is the current speed: error
$<0.01\,\%$ (timer quantisation and float rounding). This processing is not LTI
in the tooth domain -- the extrapolation interval $T_{\text{seg}}/2$ depends on
the speed -- but it uses the right model of the physics (acceleration constant in
time, speed periodic in angle).

\emph{Real ECUs} therefore (i) measure the \emph{segment time} (time for
$720^\circ/z=180^\circ$, i.e.\ the MA$_{30}$ in the period domain) for
torque, misfire and speed functions, synchronous to the firing, (ii) use the
short tooth time for angle extrapolation between teeth, and (iii) compensate
the known lag by prediction with an acceleration estimate ($\hat n=n_{\text{seg}}
+\dot n\cdot T_{\text{seg}}/2$). Note also that the lag in \emph{samples} is
constant while the lag in \emph{time} scales with $1/n$: at \SI{6000}{rpm} the
same MA$_{30}$ lags only \SI{2.6}{ms}.
