The example system

An engine on rubber mounts

An engine of about 175 kg sits on rubber mounts with a combined stiffness of 1000 N/mm. It is a mass-spring-damper system: the input is the force x(t) in kN, the output the displacement y(t) in mm.

m·ÿ + c·ẏ + k·y = x(t)

A short blow, such as a hammer strike, makes it oscillate. The oscillation decays; its frequency is the resonance frequency f₀ = √(k/m)/2π.

Introduction · Question 1

The Fourier transform of the key signals

The Fourier transform decomposes a signal into oscillations. X(f) tells us with which amplitude |X(f)| and which phase arg X(f) the frequency f is present:

X(f) = ∫ x(t)·e−j2πft dt
x(t) = ∫ X(f)·ej2πft df

Choose a signal. Look out for three things: the impulse has a flat spectrum. Shifting changes only the phase. A wide pulse loses the high frequencies.

Fourier · synthesis

An impulse is the sum of all frequencies

If we add cosines at 1, 2, 3, … Hz, all with the same amplitude and zero phase, they cancel almost everywhere. Only at t = 0 are they all at their maximum at the same time.

δN(t) = Σk=−NN ej2πkΔf·t = 1 + 2·Σk=1N cos(2πkΔf·t)

With more frequencies the peak gets taller and narrower; the area stays 1. In the limit we get δ(t). Read the other way round: an impulse contains all frequencies equally strongly and therefore excites every resonance.

Shifting by t₀ means: every oscillation gets the phase −2πf·t₀. Then they all peak together at t₀.

Δf = 1 Hz. Discrete frequencies give a periodic signal with period 1/Δf = 1 s; one period is shown.

Fourier · synthesis

From sine synthesis to the impulse response

Now we send the same oscillations through the engine mount. Each frequency f comes out amplified by |H(f)| and shifted by φ(f). This is a property of linear systems (section “Firing forces”).

hN(t) = H(0) + 2·Σk=1N |H(kΔf)|·cos(2πkΔf·t + φ(kΔf))

The sum of the filtered oscillations is the response to the sum of the oscillations, i.e. to the impulse. That is the impulse response. Frequencies near f₀ are strongly amplified, which is why h(t) oscillates at f₀.

h(t) = ∫ H(f)·ej2πft df ⟷ H(f) = ∫ h(t)·e−j2πft dt

Impulse response and frequency response are a Fourier pair.

Question 3 · foundation

Two assumptions are enough: linear and time-invariant

Impulse response

h[n] = S{δ[n]}

The response to the unit impulse δ[n]: 1 at n = 0, 0 otherwise. Here the engine mount, sampled every 10 ms.

Time invariance

S{x[n−k]} = y[n−k]

Striking later gives the same response, just later. The mount is still the same tomorrow.

Linearity

S{a·x1 + b·x2} = a·y1 + b·y2

Striking twice as hard gives twice the displacement; two strikes give the sum of the responses. This holds as long as the rubber stays in its linear range.

Try k ≈ half an oscillation period with a = 1: the second strike stops the oscillation. With k ≈ a full period it reinforces it.

Question 2

Why? Every signal is a sum of impulses

Decompose
x[n] = Σk x[k]·δ[n−k]
Time invariance
δ[n−k] → h[n−k]
Homogeneity
x[k]·δ[n−k] → x[k]·h[n−k]
Additivity
Σk … → Σk …
y[n] = Σk x[k]·h[n−k] = (x * h)[n]

The system “knows” only h. Everything else is bookkeeping: shift, scale, add.

Discrete convolution · input view

Every sample produces an echo

Each value x[k] starts a copy of the impulse response at n = k, scaled by x[k]. The output is the sum of all echoes.

y[n] = Σk x[k]·h[n−k]

Discrete convolution · output view

Flip, shift, multiply, sum

For a fixed n, h[n−k] as a function of k is the impulse response flipped and shifted to n. Multiply by x[k], sum over k: that is y[n].

y[n] = Σk x[k]·h[n−k] = Σm h[m]·x[n−m]

The same double sum as above, just read column by column. This is how a filter computes in real time: one output value per clock tick, from the most recent inputs.

Question 4

Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency

x is a sum of oscillations with spectrum X(f). The system multiplies each of them by H(f). The sum of the results is y. Hence:

y = x * h ⟷ Y(f) = X(f)·H(f)

In the time domain every output value needs a sum over many products. In the frequency domain one multiplication per frequency is enough. With the FFT this is much faster for long signals.

On the right, y is computed twice: directly as the convolution sum and as IFFT(FFT(x)·FFT(h)). The two curves lie exactly on top of each other. The spectrum shows how the resonance of H lifts out the parts of x near f₀.

Question 5

The real impulse: the impact hammer

In modal analysis the structure is struck with an impact hammer. The contact time T depends on the hammer tip: steel is hard and short, rubber soft and long. A force sensor in the hammer measures the impulse J = ∫F dt, which we divide by (here J = 1 kN·s).

gT = pT * h = (1/T)·∫t−Tt h(τ) dτ

gT is h averaged over the contact time: smoother and delayed by T/2. In the spectrum, GT(f) = PT(f)·H(f). The pulse excites frequencies evenly only up to about 1/(3T). If f₀ lies above that, the resonance is not seen properly.

Question 5

Any response from the measured pulse response

An arbitrary force signal x(t), here a load change with a superimposed 10 Hz excitation, is split into pulses of width T:

x(t) ≈ Σk x(kT)·T·pT(t−kT)

Because the system is linear and time-invariant:

y(t) ≈ Σk x(kT)·T·gT(t−kT)

For a staircase this is exact. The error arises only because x is not a staircase. So it depends on the input, not only on h: here on the 10 Hz component near the resonance.

In practice

Often better: measure the step response

Relation

s(t) = ∫0t h(τ) dτ ⇔ h = ds/dt

The real pulse is two steps

T·gT(t) = s(t) − s(t−T)

Arbitrary input (Duhamel)

y = Σ Δxk·s(t−kT) → ∫ x′(τ)·s(t−τ) dτ

On the engine, the step is a load change: the drive torque jumps, the engine tilts in its mounts and rings out. A step has a lot of energy at small amplitude and is easy to produce.

Sheet 3.3: the speed step on the rotary knob gives the step response of MA and EMA.

Drawback: h = ds/dt amplifies the measurement noise (see the RC low-pass at the end).

Question 6

Where are the limits?

Linearity

The mount has an end stop and the rubber gets progressively stiffer. A hard strike drives it out of the linear range, and superposition no longer holds.

Bandwidth

PT(f) = sinc(fT) is zero at f = k/T. These frequencies are missing from gT. Recovering H = G/PT is unstable there.

Noise

The peak force is limited (here 50 kN), so the impulse is at most 50 kN·T. Short strikes carry little energy, and after normalising the noise becomes large.

Time invariance

Rubber gets stiffer in the cold, so f₀ rises. Hydraulic mounts change their damping with amplitude. If the system changes during the measurement, there is no longer a single impulse response: y[n] = Σ x[k]·h[n, k]. Sheet 3.3 d shows this for the tooth and time domains.

Question 7 · continuous

From sum to integral: where does the normalisation come from?

Discrete impulse

δ[n]: value 1

One sample of height 1. Thought of as a rectangle over one clock period Ts, it has area 1·Ts.

Continuous impulse

δ(t): area 1

As a rectangle of width Δ it must have height 1/Δ: pΔ(t). Its unit is 1/s.

Hence the factor Δ

x(t) ≈ Σk x(kΔ)·Δ·pΔ(t−kΔ)

Δ·pΔ is a box of height 1. Multiplied by x(kΔ) it gives one strip of the staircase with area x(kΔ)·Δ.

Units for the engine mount

x in kN, y in mm, h(t) in mm/(kN·s), dτ in s: ∫ x·h dτ has the unit mm. The sampled h[n] has the unit mm/kN.

Pulses of width Δ
x(t) ≈ Σk x(kΔ)·Δ·pΔ(t−kΔ)
Response (LTI)
y(t) ≈ Σk x(kΔ)·Δ·gΔ(t−kΔ)
Limit Δ → 0
pΔ → δ, gΔ → h, Σ(…)·Δ → ∫(…) dτ
y(t) = ∫ x(τ)·h(t−τ) dτ

The Δ becomes the dτ. Without Δ it would be a sum of heights instead of areas, and it would keep growing as Δ gets finer.

The same when sampling

A digital filter that models the engine mount computes y[n] = Σ x[k]·h[n−k] without dτ. The Ts must therefore be inside h[n]:

h[n] = Ts·h(nTs)

Then the DC gain is Σh[n] ≈ ∫h dt = H(0) = 1 mm/kN. If you forget Ts, the filter amplifies too much by a factor of 1/Ts, i.e. a hundredfold at 10 ms.

Continuous convolution

Flip, shift, take the area

Input: a force rectangle of 1 kN for 100 ms, like a short blip of the throttle. System: the engine mount.

For each t, y(t) is the area under x(τ)·h(t−τ). Because h oscillates, the product has positive and negative parts: the area is signed.

y(t) = ∫00.1 s h(t−τ) dτ = s(t) − s(t − 0.1 s)

Question 8

Infinitely long inputs

Causal: only the past counts

y[n] = Σk=−∞n x[k]·h[n−k]

Exists if h is absolutely summable (BIBO)

Σ|h[n]| < ∞ ⇒ |y[n]| ≤ max|x|·Σ|h|

FIR: finite memory

y[n] = Σm=0N−1 h[m]·x[n−m]

IIR: infinitely many terms, but recursive

y[n] = a·y[n−1] + (1−a)·x[n]

EMA from Sheet 3. You never need the whole input: each output value comes from the last N inputs or from the last output. For the engine mount Σ|h| grows like 1/ζ; with no damping at all it would not be BIBO-stable. Push a above 1 and the EMA becomes unstable too.

Question 8 · frequency

Firing forces: transient and eigenfunction

The gas forces of a four-cylinder engine excite it at the 2nd engine order: f = 2·nmot/60. If the excitation has been running forever, an oscillation of the same frequency comes out:

ej2πft → H(f)·ej2πft

If it is switched on at t = 0, the system goes through a transient: the natural oscillation at f₀ decays with e−ζ·2πf₀·t, leaving the forced oscillation.

If f is near f₀, the amplitude builds up over many periods to |H| ≈ 1/(2ζ). When the engine is switched off it runs through this speed and shakes briefly.

Finally · practical example

RC low-pass: measure the impulse response, reconstruct the rectangle response

An RC low-pass with R = 1 kΩ and C = 10 µF has τ = RC = 10 ms and the cutoff frequency fc = 1/(2πτ) ≈ 16 Hz. Its model is

h(t) = (1/τ)·e−t/τ, H(f) = 1/(1 + j2πfτ)

Task: predict the response to a 5 V rectangle lasting 30 ms, using only a measured impulse response. For that we first have to get h. There are four ways, all with measurement noise on the oscilloscope:

Model

Compute h from the schematic. Exact if the component values are right.

Short pulse

Function generator: a 10 V pulse of width Tp, divide the response by 10 V·Tp. Short pulse: little energy, a lot of noise. Long pulse: smeared h.

Differentiate the step

Apply a 5 V step, measure s(t), h = ds/dt. Differentiating amplifies the noise; a moving average (Sheet 3) smooths it again but smears the start.

Noise + FFT

Apply white noise x, measure y, average H = Y·X* / |X|² over M measurements, then h = IFFT(H). This uses the convolution theorem.
